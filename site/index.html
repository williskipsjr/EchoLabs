Lift the vocals from one song. Drop them on another.

EchoLabs splits two songs into stems, stretches the vocals beat by beat onto the other song's instrumental and shifts them into a matching key. Every mashup comes with a receipt of exactly what was changed.

    How a mashup gets made

    Scroll through one real run: the first four bars of Gabriel Shellington's vocals landing on MalreDeszik's beat. Every line in the picture is the actual audio and the actual beat grid.

    1. 01

      Separate

      A neural network called Demucs pulls each song apart into four stems: vocals, drums, bass and everything else.

      • Vocals
      • Drums
      • Bass
      • Everything else
    2. 02

      Find the beat and the key

      Beats are tracked on the drums alone, so the singing can't smear them. The key is read from the bass and harmony. This song: 140 BPM in G minor.

    3. 03

      Match the keys

      The other song is in E minor. On the Camelot wheel, the DJ's map of keys that blend, the vocals need to move to the same spot, so EchoLabs picks the smallest pitch shift that gets them there.

    4. 04

      Warp beat by beat

      Real drummers drift. Instead of stretching the whole vocal by one number, EchoLabs stretches every beat onto the matching beat of the new song. Here each beat gets its own stretch.

      • Naive overlay
      • One global stretch
      • EchoLabs beat-warp
    5. 05

      Mix and credit

      The vocals sit on the new instrumental, which dips slightly in the mid-range while someone sings. Loudness is matched, and a receipt records both sources and every change.

    What didn't work, and the limits

    • A newer neural beat tracker (Beat This!) did not beat the classic one in this test, so it stays optional.
    • Key detection scores 0.588 on GiantSteps. Learned key models reach about 0.74, so this is a solid baseline, not the state of the art.
    • The benchmarks use 7-second clips, which makes the beat test noisy. That is why the ceiling is shown.

    Credit travels with the mix

    A mashup is built on other people's work. Every render writes a provenance file that names both source recordings by file hash, lists what was detected and what was changed, and marks the result as AI-generated.

    The app won't start a mashup until you confirm you have the rights to both songs. The demo uses two Creative Commons tracks from ccMixter.

    Make your own

    In your browser

    Open the notebook in Google Colab, run it, and you get a link to the full app. Upload two songs and download the mashup with its receipt. A free Colab GPU takes about a minute per mashup.

    Open in Colab

    On your machine

    Python 3.11 and uv. No GPU needed.

    git clone https://github.com/williskipsjr/EchoLabs
cd EchoLabs && uv sync
uv run echolabs mashup vocals.mp3 beat.mp3 -o mashup.wav
